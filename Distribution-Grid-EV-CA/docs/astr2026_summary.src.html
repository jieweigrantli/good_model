At a glance

The study asks how much of the load that California's delivery network cannot serve once EV charging is added can be recovered by batteries at substations, compared with building transmission. The model is a linear program: a substation-level network of the three investor-owned utilities, nested inside a balancing-area model of the Western Interconnection, dispatched over four seasonal weeks with the grid as built.

The model

Structure. One linear program with two tiers. The upper tier is 16 balancing areas of the Western Interconnection, ten outside California and six inside. The lower tier replaces PG&E, SCE and SDG&E with their substations: 2,387 nodes and 3,778 corridors. LADWP, BANC and IID stay as one bus each. Each nested utility keeps a bus for the rest of its system, reached from the substations through 195 gateways.

Physics. Transmission is a transport model: each corridor carries up to its rating in either direction, with losses, and nothing enforces Kirchhoff's voltage law. A substation's net import over its lines is capped by its transformer rating, so power passing through at transmission voltage is not limited and power stepped down to local load is. Voltage, reactive power and contingencies are not represented.

Dispatch. The grid is taken as built: no capacity can be added, apart from the storage each scenario prescribes. Load that cannot be delivered is unserved at $10,000/MWh. Wind and solar output above what the network can take is spilled. Storage is 90% efficient and its state of charge is continuous across all 672 hours.

Horizon. The first week of March, June, September and December, solved as one problem of 672 hours. The renewable standards are budgets over the whole horizon, so the weeks cannot be solved separately.

Size. 3.4 million rows, 12.0 million columns, 28.7 million non-zeros. Gurobi's barrier method solves a scenario in 7 to 10 minutes; the scenario with a battery on every EV substation takes 30.

What is compared

Each intervention is compared with S1, the EV load on the grid as built. The first measure is load served. The second is CO2, and it needs a convention, because an intervention that serves load S1 could not serve also emits for that load.

ConventionDefinitionWhat it answers
ConsequentialE(S1) − E(X)Total emissions, with the extra load's emissions charged to the intervention.

All four are saved for every scenario, with hourly generation, fuel burned and CO2 by fuel, so emission factors can be changed without solving again.

The network

WECC does not publish transmission topology, so connectivity is rebuilt from line geometry and checked against the sources that name line terminals. Statewide the build holds 3,105 substations and 4,944 corridors. The three nested utilities account for 2,387 and 3,778.

How the topology is built

  • Line endpoints snap to a substation within 1,500 m, and only to one rated at or above the line's voltage.
  • A line that passes through a substation is split there. Without that step a substation sitting on the wire was invisible, because only endpoints were examined.
  • Runs of pass-through geometry are contracted to corridors along a minimum spanning tree. Joining every pair of terminals produced corridors that do not exist, one of them 499 km long.
  • HIFLD names both terminals of lines of 100 kV and above, and OpenStreetMap links lines through shared nodes. Where either agrees with the geometry the corridor is confirmed; where it names a pair the geometry missed, the corridor is added.
  • A component that no source connects gets a tagged radial feed, sized to the larger of its peak load and its generation. 247 of the 3,778 corridors are such feeds.
  • Corridor ratings come from a voltage lookup: 500 kV is 1,500 MVA, 345 is 1,000, 230 is 400, 161 is 250, 138 is 200, 115 is 150, 69 is 80 and 60 is 60.
  • Plants of 100 MW or more keep their own switchyard as a node. A large plant further than 1 km from any substation is moved to the nearest one within 15 km whose voltage can collect it.

Three readings matter more than the rows. Corridor capacity is measured nowhere. No public source gives thermal ratings, so the layer that sets corridor congestion is the least grounded one. Base load is measured in PG&E and SCE and allocated in SDG&E. In San Diego the transformer limit compares an allocated load with a published rating, and nothing arbitrates when they disagree. The municipal utilities publish no capacity data and will not; Li and Jenn left them out for the same reason.

Load, fleet and policy

Load

EV charging goes from travel zone to census block to feeder to substation, following Li and Jenn: home charging by block population, workplace and public charging by jobs, blocks to feeders by intersection, feeders to substations by the utility's own records. Assignments are masked to the assigning utility's territory, which removed 190,139 cross-territory assignments.

Base load is the utility's published substation profile where one exists. Only the remainder, the area total less the measured profiles, is allocated, and it goes to the utility's bus and not onto unmeasured substations. Among unmeasured neighbours within 5 km, base load is split by published rating. EV load is never reallocated.

In the four weeks the three utilities peak at 41.9 GW together. EV charging peaks at 3.3 GW and is 8.0% of their energy, about 17 TWh a year.

The 2023 fleet

The fleet GOOD ships for the West is EPA's IPM v6.17 list of existing units, about 2018, plus 78 plants that model projected would be built by 2023, with all wind on the best resource class at a 50% capacity factor. It gave California 58 TWh of wind a year against 14 actual. 08_19 now compares the list with eGRID 2023, generator by generator: projected plants are removed, units no longer operating are removed, missing plants are added, and each region's wind and solar profile is set to the capacity factor its plants had in 2023.

Emission factors

Each plant's CO2 rate is read from the raw eGRID 2023 plant file by plant code. Coal, gas and oil plants with no usable rate take heat rate times EIA carbon content. As dispatched, coal emits 1,061 kg/MWh and gas 454. Four other factor sets are computed beside this one, and the range across them is saved with every result.

Renewable portfolio standards

GOOD's policy file writes each state standard as a share of in-state generation with no shortfall allowed. That form suits capacity planning. With nothing allowed to be built it becomes a cap on in-state gas and coal: on the older fleet California's non-renewable output was the same 10,629 GWh in every scenario. Two alternatives have been run, and neither is yet the default.

  • Published form with a shortfall price of $50/MWh, the CPUC penalty. The model can now break the cap by paying.
  • On load served, which is what the ratios mean in their source. In as-built dispatch nothing the solver controls changes compliance, so the standard is reported and does not constrain the solve.

Runs on the 2023 fleet use the 2023 ratios (California 39.3%, against 44.8% for 2025).

Where load goes unserved, and which corridors bind

{{frag:unserved_bars}}

Unserved load in GWh. Storage on the unserved load removes 49% of it. Corridor relief removes 62%, and the rest goes only when transformers are relaxed too, so every unserved MWh in S1 is attributable to one limit or the other. Storage on every EV substation removes 7%, because most of it sits where nothing is short. Storage on the spill removes none: no substation has both spill and unserved load.

The transformer-bound remainder is a data conflict. 32.8 of the 32.9 GWh left in S3 is at 44 substations whose published or ICA rating is below their peak load. Wabash in San Diego carries more than half of it: a published 16.9 MW against 72 MW of allocated load, short in all 672 hours. SDG&E publishes no substation load to settle which number is wrong. This quantity should be reported as a conflict between two data sources and kept out of the congestion estimate.

Where the storage sits

Where wind and solar are spilled

Scaled to a year the spill is about 7.5 TWh, against roughly 2.6 TWh the California ISO curtailed in 2023 (EIA). 225 GWh of it is at 32 substations where the plants attached exceed the outgoing lines by 9.1 GW in total. Most are solar and storage plants built after the substation data was compiled, whose own collector stations are missing from it, so they landed on the nearest small substation. Hinkley alone spills 86 GWh: 536 MW of solar behind 60 MW of line. The other 317 GWh is behind a limit further along and has not been separated into real congestion and the same fault one step removed.

Does the model run plants the way they ran?

The no-EV scenario scaled to a year, against what eGRID 2023 reports. The small figure is modelled over actual.

{{frag:dispatch_table}}

The fleet update put wind within 4% of actual and solar within 12% under either standard. The two standards then pull thermal dispatch in opposite directions, and neither reproduces 2023.

  • On load served, coal is 5% high and gas 29% low across the West. California burns 29% more gas than it did and exports 5% of its load, where it imports at least 17%.
  • In the published form at $50/MWh, the standard binds harder on the 2023 fleet, which has a quarter of the California wind the IPM fleet had. California's gas falls to 60% of actual and the state imports 19% of its load, which matches. The imports are coal: 32% above actual across the West.

Three things bias both. Hydro runs 33 TWh a year above its 2023 output and displaces gas. The model has no California carbon price. And the duplicated plants add 6 to 7 TWh a year of California gas, while 8.8 TWh of IPM's import is counted as California generation.

Known and not yet fixed

Plants counted twice

156 plants, 3,285 MW (1,920 MW of gas, 694 of solar, 446 of oil), are filed under LADWP or BANC but sit nearest a substation of a nested utility. The nesting step attaches them to that substation and also leaves them on their own area's bus. In S1 the two copies generate 874 and 719 GWh, so about 1.2% of all generation comes from a second copy of capacity that exists once. It is in all five configurations, at about the same size.

Fix: keep one copy. A plant filed under an area that is not nested stays on that area's bus. A small change, then every scenario solved again.

Plants with no substation that can collect them

64 plant sites, 17.3 GW, have no adequately rated substation within 15 km. At 32 substations attached capacity exceeds the outgoing lines by 9.1 GW. This produces at least 225 GWh of the spill and inflates the corridor and curtailment-storage emission figures.

Proposed: give each such plant a tie line of its own nameplate to the nearest substation that can collect it, since no plant is built without one.

Dispatch away from 2023

With the standard on load served, California burns 29% more gas than in 2023 and exports. In the published form it burns 40% less and the West burns 32% more coal. Hydro is 22% high in both. Causes identified so far: hydro at a long-run average, no California carbon price, the duplicated plants, and an import asset counted as in-state generation.

Plants in the wrong region

IPM drew its regions on a map. It files 120 existing plants, 9,189 MW, outside their balancing authority's regions, 73 of them California ISO plants under LADWP. New plants are placed correctly; these were left where they are.

EV adoption level not recorded

The charging input adds about 17 TWh a year in the three utilities, several times today's EV load. The year or fleet size it represents is not carried through the pipeline and has to be stated in the paper.

Limitations

LimitationEffect on results
Transport model, not DC power flowNo loop flow. Transfers that Kirchhoff's voltage law would forbid are allowed, so congestion is a lower bound.
Corridor ratings from a voltage lookupCorridor congestion rests on typical ratings, not the lines' own. Every 230 kV circuit is taken as 400 MVA.
Half the corridors inferred from geometryBelow 100 kV the topology has no independent check, and almost two thirds of corridors are below 100 kV.
SDG&E load is allocatedSan Diego carries 55% of S1's unserved load and has no measured substation load. Its largest single item is a rating conflict.
LADWP, BANC and IID as single busesNo congestion is visible inside them, and flows through them are unconstrained.
Four weeks, not a yearAnnual figures are the four weeks times 13. Seasonal extremes outside the sampled weeks are missed.
Grid as built, one EV levelResults describe one adoption level on one fleet. They are not a forecast and say nothing about how the gap grows.
Storage sized as an upper boundThe shortfall fleets are twice the measured need. They show what storage can reach, not what it costs to reach it.
Storage durations assumedFour hours for every existing battery and ten for pumped hydro, from GOOD's defaults.
No unit commitment or reservesPlants ramp freely and nothing is held back, so thermal dispatch is more flexible than in operation.
CO2 only, plant-average ratesMethane and nitrous oxide are not counted. A plant rate averages its units; 5% of fossil energy is on units whose plant rate is far from their own heat rate.

Decisions and next steps

Decisions that are yours

  • Tie lines for plants with no adequate substation. Recommended. It changes spill and both emission figures that depend on it.
  • Which form of the renewable standard is the default. The load-served form matches what the ratios mean and leaves California's gas free to respond. The published form with a price is closer to GOOD as distributed.
  • Which CO2 convention the paper reports for storage. All four are saved, so this needs no new run.
  • Whether to move the 120 plants IPM files outside their balancing authority.

Next run

Fix the double-counted plants, add tie lines if approved, rebuild the network and solve all eight scenarios again. About 90 minutes per configuration.

Improvements, roughly in order of value

  • EV adoption levels. Run the scenarios at several adoption years. The question is how the gap between storage and transmission moves as EV load grows, and one level cannot show that.
  • A source for line ratings. Utility transmission plans or interconnection studies for the corridors that bind most. 301 corridors account for the congestion; rating those is a bounded task.
  • SDG&E data. Wire in the downloaded ICA fully and send the drafted request for substation load. It would settle the Wabash conflict and half the remaining unserved load.
  • Cost. Levelised cost per MWh relieved and per tonne for storage and for corridor upgrades, on the current runs. The earlier cost comparison predates most of the fixes above.
  • Storage sized to cost, not to need. A supply curve: relief against MW installed, to find where storage stops being worth it.
  • Dispatch realism. Hydro at 2023 output, a California carbon price, and the import asset treated as an import.
  • A full year. The annual horizon instead of four weeks, once the configuration is settled.
  • DC power flow. Angle-based flow on the corridors above 100 kV, where terminals are documented.
  • SCE sub-transmission from its circuit inventory, so those corridors are asserted and not inferred.
